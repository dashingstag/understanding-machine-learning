Loss vs model size at fixed compute

Straight lines on log–log paper

Did you know?

Jared Kaplan's 2020 OpenAI paper fit the same power law across 6 orders of magnitude — the field's closest thing to a physical law (the result).

In the real world

Labs fit the exponents on cheap runs, then extrapolate to the frontier run before spending it. Loss is predicted before the model exists — usually to ~2%.

Test loss falls as a clean power of each factor: $L(N,D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}$. No theory predicted it; nothing has broken it — yet.

Chinchilla: the compute-optimal split

Did you know?

Before Chinchilla, "bigger is better" meant GPT-3 (175B) trained on 300B tokens. Hoffmann et al. showed a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beats it — Gopher's budget, one-third the size.

In the real world

The rule — D ≈ 20N — reset every lab's sizing sheet and is why a 7B model is trained on a trillion tokens. Inference costs then pushed labs past optimal (smaller, over-trained models are cheaper to serve — see inference).

At fixed compute, loss is U-shaped in model size: too big wastes FLOPs on parameters, too small wastes them on data. The panel shows the U and its minimum — the Chinchilla point.

What the law buys — and misses

Did you know?

Loss scales smoothly; capabilities needn't — Wei et al.'s emergent abilities paper showed tasks jumping at thresholds, though later work blames discontinuous metrics. Both claims are in evaluation's remit.

In the real world

The post-2024 frontier added a second curve: performance vs inference FLOPs — reasoning models scale compute at test time instead of parameters.

The law predicts loss, not skill — and it needs fresh data: repeated epochs decay quickly. It's a budgeting tool, not a capability oracle — Goodhart-proofing it is an open practice.
