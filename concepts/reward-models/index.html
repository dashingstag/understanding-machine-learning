Reward models

MapNotes

The learned proxy for human preference — a model trained on pairwise rankings that RL optimizes against.

Statement

A learned scalar proxy for human preference: a (usually transformer) head trained on pairwise rankings via Bradley–Terry — $\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$. In rlhf, the reward-r it outputs is the environment; dpo shows the same signal can be used without building the model at all.

In my own words

One paragraph, as few symbols as possible. Written by me, not copied.

Why the parent needs it

post-training — humans can't score the millions of samples RL needs; the RM is the scalable stand-in for "a person liked this". Every goodhart-law failure in alignment — hacking, sycophancy, verbosity bias — is a reward-model flaw being optimized.

Teardown

  1. Pairwise > absolute. Raters agree more on "which is better" than on scores — Bradley–Terry converts rankings to a scalar via the logistic difference.
  2. Architecture. Usually the SFT model minus the LM head plus a scalar head — it reads completions, not generates them.
  3. Calibration tricks. Ensemble RMs, margin regularization, out-of-distribution penalty terms — all patches on the same wound: the RM is only valid near its training distribution.
  4. Reward hacking. The policy exploits RM blind spots: length inflation, sycophancy, formatting tells — mitigated by kl-div penalty to the SFT policy (beta), never eliminated.
  5. Generative/verifiable alternatives. verifiable-rewards where checkable; rubric-scored judges (llm-as-judge) elsewhere; RM is the middle ground for fuzzy human taste.

Worked example

RM score spread: chosen $r = 2.1$, rejected $r = -0.4$ on prompt $x$.

  • Pair loss contribution: $-\log\sigma(2.1 - (-0.4)) = -\log\sigma(2.5) \approx 0.078$ — already confident.
  • If the policy finds an exploit producing $r = 8$ on gibberish, nothing in the RM stops it — the KL leash and OOD penalties are the only brakes. That's reward hacking in one number.

Open questions
