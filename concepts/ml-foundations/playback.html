The training loop, forever

The substrate under everything

Did you know?

Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron in 1958 as literal hardware — weights were motor-driven potentiometers wired by hand. Its single-layer limits, attacked in 1969, helped trigger the first AI winter.

In the real world

Every page on this map compiles to the same five moves on the right — a $200M training run and a homework logistic regression differ in scale, not in kind.

Strip any modern ML system down and five ideas remain: a model (a parameterized function), a loss (one scalar saying how wrong it is), data (what it sees), a descent rule (how it improves), and generalization (the only honest test).

This page is the foundations' own overview — each pillar has its own node: gradient descent, backpropagation and optimizers.

Pillar 1 — a model is a function

Did you know?

The universal approximation theorem says even a single hidden layer can compute any continuous function — in principle. Depth is what makes it practical, which is why everything here is deep.

$f_\theta$: sequence of token ids in, distribution over the next token out. All the learning lives in model parameters $\theta$ — for a frontier model, a vector with $\sim 10^{11}$ components.

The function's shape is the architecture — Transformer architecture for language, diffusion denoisers for images. The shape is chosen; the weights are learned.

Pillar 4 — gradients are manufactured

Did you know?

David Rumelhart's 1986 Nature paper (with Hinton & Williams) is the citation behind every gradient on this map — though the chain-rule trick was published in control theory a decade earlier.

$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}$ has hundreds of billions of entries — nobody computes it by hand. Backpropagation runs the chain rule backward through the computation graph: each layer hands its gradient signal to the layer before it, at the cost of roughly two forward passes.

It's why a model with a trillion parameters still gets a usable gradient every step — the single most load-bearing algorithm in the field.

Pillar 5 — the only score that counts

Did you know?

Textbooks said models fail by overfitting — bigger models should get worse. Double descent showed error can fall, spike at perfect-fit, then fall again — overturning the intuition on its own turf.

In the real world

'Memorized vs learned' is now a product question: regurgitating training data verbatim is both an eval failure and a legal one. See evaluation and benchmark contamination.

Fitting the data is easy — a hash table does that. The whole enterprise only pays off if $f_\theta$ performs on inputs it never saw. Held-out data, honest evaluation and the gap between train and test loss are how we find out.

Embeddings deserve a word too: between raw input and the stack sit learned vectors — geometry is the fifth language of the foundations, after probability, linear algebra and optimization.
