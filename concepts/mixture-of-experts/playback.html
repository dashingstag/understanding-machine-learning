Mixture of experts

Router: tokens pick experts

One FFN becomes a menu of them

Did you know?

Noam Shazeer published the idea in 2017 — a 137B-parameter LSTM with 2 experts per token — and it stayed a curiosity until Mixtral and DeepSeek made sparse the default.

In the real world

Mixtral-8×7B: 47B params, ~13B active → dense-13B price, dense-40B quality. DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B active. The trick is why open models keep pace.

Replace each FFN with $E$ copies plus a router: every token visits only its top-$k$ experts. Parameters multiply; per-token compute doesn't — `moe-layer`.

The router is the whole invention

Did you know?

The router is a softmax over experts — a tiny linear layer — but getting a discrete top-$k$ choice to be trainable is the trick: the gate score becomes the expert's weight in the sum, so gradients flow through it.

In the real world

Load imbalance is the production problem: tokens collapse onto a few experts → dropped tokens, wasted hardware. Auxiliary losses (Switch) or bias adjustments (DeepSeek) fight it — details.

Each token gets a gate score vector; the top-$k$ winners mix. The panel shows different tokens routing to different expert pairs — "the", "def", "∫" all take different doors.

What sparsity buys — and costs

Did you know?

Experts do specialize — but not cleanly: "math expert" labels mostly fail on inspection; specialization is more statistical than semantic. An interpretability question, open.

In the real world

Cheap FLOPs, expensive bytes: all $E$ experts must live in memory even though each token uses $k$. MoE shifts the bill from compute to memory+bandwidth — an inference and parallelism problem, not an accuracy one.

Capacity grows ~$E\times$; active compute stays dense-scale. The cost: full parameter residency, routing complexity, and a capacity factor that can silently drop tokens.
