Descent on a quadratic bowl

The question: how do you improve a billion knobs?

Did you know?

Cauchy proposed steepest descent in 1847 — for hand computation. Stochastic descent followed in 1951 (Robbins–Monro): the same two ideas still run every frontier training run.

In the real world

There is no plan B. RLHF, DPO, MoE routing, tokenizer-free models — every learned thing in this map is produced by this update or a variant of it.

A modern model's model parameters $\theta$ number in the hundreds of billions. Trial and error is out; calculus is in. For each knob we can ask: does nudging it up raise or lower the loss? That answer — for all of them at once — is the gradient.

Statement

Did you know?

The update rule is one line; the machinery that makes it usable is backpropagation — the gradient factory nobody sees.

Three roles, three failure modes: $\theta$ is huge (cost), $\eta$ is tiny (stability), and $\nabla\mathcal{L}$ is estimated on a minibatch (noise). The rest of this page is managing those three.

Step size rules everything

In the real world

When a frontier run's loss spikes at 3 a.m., the first thing engineers touch is the learning rate schedule — warmup length, peak, decay shape. It is the most-watched dial in training.

Too small: convergence never arrives inside the compute budget. Too large: the step overshoots the valley, the next gradient is larger still, and the loss explodes — the toy on the right shows both regimes in one step.

The standard fix is a schedule: a short warmup while the model is still chaotic, a plateau, then a decay (cosine or linear) that anneals into the minimum.

Stochastic, momentum, adaptive

Did you know?

Diederik Kingma co-authored Adam in 2014 — two running averages (of the gradient and its square) that became the default optimizer — and separately the VAE and flow matching. Productive decade.

In the real world

Nobody computes the full-data gradient — minibatch SGD is noisier per step and massively cheaper per pass over training data, and the noise doubles as exploration.

Three upgrades on the naive rule: minibatches (cheap noisy gradients, better generalization), momentum (keep a velocity; roll past small bumps), adaptive rates (per-parameter steps — Adam). All torn down in optimizers.

Try it — one parameter, one knob

Did you know?

On a quadratic, plain descent converges geometrically — error × (1 − 2ηa) each step where $a$ is the curvature. Above a critical $\eta$ the multiplier flips sign and grows: divergence is a cliff, not a slope.

In the real world

Same drama, real stakes: a too-hot LR on a $10M run doesn't politely warn you — the loss curve spikes and the checkpoint is garbage.
