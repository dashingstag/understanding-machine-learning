Signal vs depth

The skip connection

Did you know?

ResNet (He et al. 2015) fixed a 152-layer CNN with one line: $x + f(x)$. The transformer borrowed it verbatim — depth stopped being a cliff.

In the real world

Without the skip path, gradient descent sees gradients multiplied by per-layer gain ~96 times — vanish or explode, pick your poison. The panel plots it.

Each sublayer becomes $x \mapsto x + f(\mathrm{Norm}(x))$: the layer proposes an edit, never a rewrite. Gradients always have an identity path home — that alone is most of why 100-layer stacks train at all.

The residual stream

Did you know?

Chris Olah's Anthropic team reframed the stream as a shared communication channel — every attention head and FFN "publishes" into it. That framing made mechanistic interpretability a field.

In the real world

Activation steering exploits the stream's linearity: add a "refusal" or "honesty" direction mid-forward-pass and the behaviour changes — no retraining.

Because every sublayer reads and writes the same vector, the hidden state is a running draft — attention deposits gathered context, the FFN deposits computed features. Reading the draft is interpretability; editing it is control.

LayerNorm and RMSNorm

Did you know?

Pre-norm vs post-norm decided whether transformers could be deep: post-norm (original paper) makes the early layers' warmup brutal; pre-norm — norm inside the branch — became universal in GPT/Llama-era stacks.

LayerNorm re-centers each position to mean 0, variance 1, then rescales by learned $\gamma,\beta$ — see `layernorm` for the formula. RMSNorm skips centering entirely; same stability, less compute — why Llama and friends chose it.
