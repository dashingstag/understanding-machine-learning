Who looks at whom

The problem: tokens can't see each other

Did you know?

The seq2seq models that Ilya Sutskever co-invented squeezed a whole sentence into one fixed vector — the bottleneck Dzmitry Bahdanau's alignment scores were invented to open (2014).

In the real world

In a frontier data centre, the QKᵀ and weights·V matmuls run trillions of times a day — attention is among the most-executed learned operations in production anywhere.

Tokens arrive as embeddings — independent rows produced by tokenization plus a lookup table. As a bag of vectors, "the cat sat" and "sat the cat" are the same. Reading a sentence needs an operation that lets positions exchange information.

Before 2017 that op was recurrence (read left to right, carry a state — serial and forgetful over distance) or convolution (a fixed window — blind beyond it). The Transformer architecture replaced both with a single learned mixing step: attention.

Statement

Did you know?

Attention Is All You Need (2017) was a machine-translation paper with a provocative title — Ashish Vaswani and seven co-authors removed recurrence entirely, and the architecture quietly became the default for everything.

Each input row is projected three ways into a query matrix, a key matrix and a value matrix; scores are divided by the square root of the key dimension, softmaxed, and used to blend the values. The projection weights live inside model parameters $\theta$ — learned during pretraining, fixed thereafter.

Q, K, V — ask, advertise, carry

Did you know?

In the original 512-wide transformer the attention block holds about half the parameters of the feed-forward block — the mixing op is the cheap part of the layer (Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.4/table equivalents).

In the real world

During generation, past K and V rows are cached so they are never recomputed — the KV cache is the dominant memory cost of serving and a star of inference and efficiency.

The same token row $x_i$ produces all three vectors — the only difference is which learned weight matrix multiplies it:

  • query $q_i = x_i\mathbf{W}^Q$ — what this token is looking for ("does anything here modify me?")
  • key $k_j = x_j\mathbf{W}^K$ — what this token advertises ("match me if you need a subject / a number / a pronoun")
  • value $v_j = x_j\mathbf{W}^V$ — the content actually passed on when a query matches

Nothing about $q_i \cdot k_j$ is symmetric or ordered — relevance is directed: "sat" may care strongly about "cat" while "cat" barely looks back.

Scores → scale → softmax

Did you know?

The $\sqrt{d_k}$ factor looks cosmetic but isn't: this repo's playground/attention_toy.py shows the same scores pushed visibly nearer one-hot without it — and the gap widens as $d_k$ grows.

In the real world

It is the same lever as a chat UI's temperature: multiplying logits before the softmax sharpens or flattens a distribution — here the multiplier is fixed at $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ to keep gradients flowing.

$\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}$ is an $n \times n$ score matrix — entry $(i,j)$ is $q_i \cdot k_j$. With unit-variance components the dot product's variance grows like $d_k$, so the paper divides by $\sqrt{d_k}$ to hold it near 1 (Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.2.1).

softmax then normalises each row into weights summing to 1 — a per-token probability distribution over where to read from.

Masking and multiple heads

Did you know?

Heads aren't identical copies — Voita et al. (2019) pruned most heads of a trained model with little loss, and the survivors carried identifiable jobs: positional, syntactic, rare-word.

In the real world

Causal masking is why every chat model generates left to right: a decoder trained with future positions masked to $-\infty$ simply has no score to attend to beyond the cursor.

Mask. Before the softmax, disallowed positions are set to $-\infty$ — their weight becomes exactly 0. The causal mask ($j > i$ forbidden) is what makes next-token pretraining well-defined, and it is still how nearly all frontier LLMs train.

Why it won — and what it costs

Did you know?

The 2017 transformer was a 6-encoder + 6-decoder translation model — no chat interface, no agents, a very specific benchmark it beat while training in parallel for the first time.

In the real world

"Million-token context" announcements are engineering feats precisely because of this frame: sparse, sliding-window and linear-attention variants all trade away pieces of the exact $n \times n$ computation.

Attention replaced recurrence because it is parallel over the sequence: every score is an independent matmul entry, so a GPU computes a whole layer at once. That parallelism is what let training runs scale — the feed-forward of scaling laws.

The price: self-attention is O(n²) in sequence length — every token scores every token. Long documents, long agent transcripts and long reasoning and test-time compute traces all slam into that wall, which is why so much of inference and efficiency is one long workaround for this line.
